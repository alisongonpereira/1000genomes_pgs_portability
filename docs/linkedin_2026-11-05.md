## C. Execution plan

The activity should produce a three-panel figure using simulated data, explicitly labeled as such.

1. **Panel A:** show two hypothetical raw-PRS distributions with distinct means and spreads.
2. **Panel B:** apply a global transformation, $Z = (S - \mu_{\mathrm{global}})/\sigma_{\mathrm{global}}$, showing that it preserves individuals' ordering and the standardized difference between populations, even though it changes the numerical units.
3. **Panel C:** apply $Z = (S - \mu_{\mathrm{group}})/\sigma_{\mathrm{group}}$ separately per population, showing that means become zero and standard deviations become one by construction, without this constituting evidence of an absence of genetic or risk differences.

The figure should be accompanied by an explanation of which metrics are invariant to common affine transformations and which comparisons stop meaning the same thing after group-specific standardization. The deliverable is a public notebook with code, parameters, random seeds, and a scientific caption. No institutional or patient data will be used.

## D. English-language publication — draft

> Normalization can change a PRS distribution without changing the genetic information.
>
> When comparing polygenic risk scores across populations, the choice of normalization reference matters. But not every change in the appearance of a distribution represents a change in the underlying genetic information.
>
> Consider three representations of the same scores: raw values, globally standardized values, and values standardized separately within each reference population.
>
> A common linear transformation preserves individual rankings and standardized between-population differences. Population-specific standardization, however, forces each reference group to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one. Differences in group means and variances disappear by construction.
>
> This does not demonstrate that the original differences were technical artifacts, nor does it establish that the populations have equivalent disease risk.
>
> It illustrates a distinction that matters in computational genomics: a transformation can change the statistical question without changing the genotypes or the PRS weights.
>
> Before interpreting population-level PRS distributions, we need to specify what was calculated, which reference population was used, and which properties of the scores were preserved or removed by normalization.
>
> Distributional comparability, predictive validity, and clinical calibration remain separate questions.

## F. Strategic critique

A post that merely states that ancestry influences PRS would add little to scientific positioning. The differentiator here is demonstrating a precise statistical property and explaining its consequences for the interpretation of results.

There is also an important limit: normalization is not synonymous with bias correction. A transformation that eliminates differences between population means does not demonstrate that predictive bias was removed. Without phenotypes, it is not possible to assess discrimination or clinical calibration.

The recommendation is to finish the reproducible demonstration first and publish the figure and text afterward. The next step in the portfolio should favor verifiable professional experience rather than immediately repeating another PRS explainer.
